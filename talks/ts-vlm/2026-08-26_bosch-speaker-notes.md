# Bosch 25 min — what to say

~1 min per slide (~120 words). Point at the figure; don’t recite the cards. **Stop at 23.** Skip **21** if you’re late. **24–25** only if they ask.

---

1. Slide 1 — opening.
    “Let me start with the basic idea. On the left is the usual representation of a time series: you immediately see amplitude, trend, and the axes. On the right is a delay embedding of exactly the same signal. It deliberately gives up absolute scale, but exposes shape, recurrence, and topology. My question was: can I give both representations to a vision-language model and train one model that can look at a series and answer questions about it?”
2. Slide 2 — what ‘reasoning’ means.
    “And by reasoning, I mean something fairly concrete. Sometimes I want the model to describe one window: trend, seasonality, anomalies, regime changes. Sometimes I want it to compare two windows. And sometimes I want it to relate events—to identify what happened first, or what one pattern implies about another. So this is broader than forecasting a fixed horizon. The output is an answer about the signal.”
3. Slide 3 — evaluation.
    “I wanted the evaluation to come from existing benchmarks rather than from questions I designed myself. TSExam tests fairly controlled, exam-style understanding. ChatTS is open-ended and much more sensitive to numerical accuracy. And TSRBench is the hardest: questions like this require the model to first locate several events and then reason about their temporal relationship. I’ll come back to the actual numbers later.”
4. Slide 4 — related work.
    “There are roughly three ways people connect time series to language models. One is simply to serialize the numbers as text. Another is to learn a native time-series encoder and insert its representations into the LLM. The third is to render the signal and use a VLM. I’m in this third family, but with an important difference: I use two complementary geometries of the signal—a conventional chart and a delay representation.”
5. Slide 5 — why not text.
    “The first approach becomes problematic very quickly with length. These OpenTSLM experiments are actually on learned SoftPrompt tokens, not raw digits, and even there memory grows rapidly with sequence length. Raw serialization can be worse. A multichannel sensor can easily turn into tens of thousands of language tokens. Apart from memory, models also start behaving poorly on long numeric strings. So I didn’t want the time series to occupy the language context in this form.”
6. Slide 6 — native TS encoders.
    “The standard solution is to compress the sequence first. You divide it into windows, encode those windows, and give the resulting tokens to the language model. This is a perfectly reasonable approach and usually much better than dumping digits. The tradeoff I cared about is architectural: now I own a specialized time-series interface. I don’t automatically inherit improvements in pretrained vision encoders and VLM instruction tuning. My alternative was to keep the signal in a modality the backbone already understands.”
7. Slide 7 — roadmap.
    “That led to four practical problems, and these are the four parts of the rest of the talk. First, the available time-series/text data are not really sufficient for the kind of reasoning I want. Second, I need multiple views and multiple channels to survive correctly through the architecture. Third, there is a training-order problem—what should learn when? And finally, I wanted to evaluate against the existing protocols rather than a private test.”
8. Slide 8 — source datasets.
    “These are the three sources I started from. TSExam gives me controlled synthetic objects and compositions. ChatTS has a richer attribute pool and generates both signals and questions from those attributes. CaTS is different: these are real windows from eleven domains, with metadata and captions. I extend the first two because I can control their generators. CaTS I use as-is.”
9. Slide 9 — gold captions.
    “The first extension is deliberately simple: before asking the model to reason, I want it to reliably identify what it sees. Here I know that the signal contains a linear trend, a sine with a particular period and amplitude, and Gaussian noise because those are the parameters I sampled. So the supervision comes directly from the generator. There’s no LLM generating a label and no estimator trying to recover the parameters afterward.”
10. Slide 10 — gold reasoning.
    “Once I have that machinery, I can generate reasoning examples whose answers are also known by construction. Here, series two is a shifted copy of series one, and I know the exact injected lag. In the second example, I literally concatenated trend, sine, and flat segments in that order. So I can supervise not only the final answer but also a short reasoning trace whose factual content is guaranteed by the generator.”
11. Slide 11 — architecture.
    “Architecturally, there is still only one language model doing the reasoning. Every time-series marker expands into two visual spans. The conventional chart goes through Qwen’s existing vision encoder. The delay view goes through a separate DINOv3 tower. Those representations are interleaved into the same question and decoded by the same language model. So this is not an ensemble of two reasoners—it is one reasoner receiving two views.”
12. Slide 12 — why two towers.
    “The reason for keeping both views is empirical. Delay embeddings intentionally remove scale, so on numerical ChatTS questions they perform badly by themselves. But on some structural categories—anomalies, for example—they beat the chart. The failures are complementary. And when I feed exactly the same delay images through Qwen’s native vision tower instead of DINO, performance drops substantially. So this wasn’t just a question of adding another picture; the second geometry benefited from its own encoder.”
13. Slide 13 — multivariate handling.
    “There is also a less glamorous but important engineering issue here. A multivariate signal cannot simply be flattened. If I take a [channels, time] array and ravel it, I have created one artificial sequence in which the end of one sensor is followed by the beginning of another. That destroys the semantics of the delay representation. So each named channel gets its own marker, chart, and delay view. I explicitly enforce that contract in the collator.”
14. Slide 14 — training curriculum.
    “The training recipe ended up having three stages. First, perception: teach the additional visual pathway what a time series looks like while leaving the language model alone. Second, reasoning: merge that capability and train the language model on questions and answers. Third, preference optimization—but only once the model has meaningful reasoning traces to optimize. The broad shape resembles multimodal curricula such as LLaVA, but the data and transition criteria here are specific to this problem.”
15. Slide 15 — Stage A.
    “This first stage turned out to be essential. I spent a lot of time trying instruction-only variants, and performance kept stalling around 62%. Qualitatively, the model could produce perfectly reasonable language—it simply had not learned the new visual representation well enough. So in Stage A I freeze the language model, keep the chart encoder frozen, and train the delay pathway on captions. The task is deliberately perception-only: identify the components before learning exam behavior.”
16. Slide 16 — Stage B.
    “Then I merge Stage A and change the objective completely. Now the target is an answer rather than a description. The language model receives LoRA adaptation and learns MCQ, open QA, and numerical questions. On the 8B generalist I keep the vision stack fixed; in some of the larger campaigns I also adapt DINO. For part of the exam mixture I include a short trace—first explain the evidence, then produce the answer.”
17. Slide 17 — Stage C.
    “Preference optimization was actually a useful negative result. If SFT already gives eight identical correct letters, GRPO sees essentially no reward variance and has nothing useful to optimize. That is what happened here. Worse, rewarding the appearance of reasoning before the reasoning was trustworthy made performance drop. What helped was first training on traces whose content I know is correct from the generators. Only after that does preference optimization become useful for things like output structure. This stage is still in progress.”
18. Slide 18 — headline results.
    “These are the headline results, and I’ve kept the published evaluation protocols rather than putting everything through a new harness. On TSExam, the 8B model reaches 92.6%, above the GPT-4o number reported by that paper. On ChatTS, the 27B model is above the published ChatTS-14B categorical results. TSRBench is more sobering: the 8B model is roughly tied with the open 32B Qwen VLM, but GPT-5 is still about ten points ahead. So the open-model result is competitive, but there is clearly unresolved headroom.”
19. Slide 19 — where the gap is.
    “The TSRBench breakdown tells me where that headroom is. Perception is comparatively strong at 0.87, so simply reading the rendered signal is not the dominant problem anymore. Prediction is around 0.50 and decision around 0.37. The weakest group is reasoning at 0.29, especially etiological and temporal-relation questions. So my diagnosis is not ‘we need a better chart reader.’ It is that the model is missing reasoning primitives needed after it has seen the events.”
20. Slide 20 — experimental discipline.
    “This is one experiment I like because the aggregate metric would have told me to keep it. The overall reasoning average improved, and two categories went up by roughly seven points. But temporal relation—the slice I had chosen in advance as the target—fell from 26.9 to 21.9. That crossed a kill criterion I had written before the run. So I stopped adding more of the same data and went back to inspect what operators were missing from the generators.”
21. Slide 21 — scale.
    “One thing I learned is that these results are not a clean model-size ladder. I use the 0.8B model to test recipes cheaply. The 8B model still gives my best TSExam and TSRBench result. The 27B model is particularly good on the more language-heavy ChatTS benchmark. But simply moving to 9B or 27B does not fix TSRBench. That reinforces the diagnosis from the previous slides: this looks more like a data-and-reasoning problem than a parameter-count problem.”
22. Slide 22 — Bosch transfer.
    “This is where I think the idea becomes interesting for sensor systems. I would not claim that this checkpoint already understands Bosch microphones or vibration sensors—I haven’t trained that experiment. The architectural idea is that the renderer can reflect the relevant physics. For these series I use chart and delay views. For vibration or audio, an STFT may be much more informative. You can preserve the same VLM backbone while changing the visual representation presented to it.”
23. Slide 23 — close.
    “So the four things I’d leave you with are these. First, for time-series reasoning I needed supervision whose semantics I could trust, so I extended controlled generators rather than relying entirely on synthetic LLM labels. Second, complementary representations helped, but they still feed one reasoner. Third, training order mattered: learn to see, then answer, and only then optimize preferences. And finally, the open models are competitive overall, but the hard reasoning slice remains very clearly unsolved. I’m happy to go deeper on the architecture, data, or evaluation.”

24. Slide 24 — panel only.
    “For heterogeneous sensors, I would unify at the backbone rather than forcing the raw signals into one representation. A microphone and slow telemetry do not need the same renderer or sampling rate. I might use an STFT for one and chart or delay features for the other, while sharing the VLM downstream. And I definitely would not upsample the slow sensor onto the audio clock simply to make the tensors look uniform. Preserve the structure first; fuse at an appropriate level.”
25. Slide 25 — panel only.
    “This is the sampling-rate version of the same principle. The naive approach is to force everything onto one clock. Audio is naturally dense, while slow telemetry becomes an interpolated staircase; now you’ve inflated the sequence without creating information, and the two modalities still have very different structure. What I would do instead is preserve each sensor at its native rate and give it an appropriate representation—for example, an STFT or short windows for audio, and chart or delay views for slower telemetry. Then fuse after encoding, either on a coarser shared timeline or through cross-attention. So the unification happens in the backbone, not by pretending all sensors have the same clock. I haven’t yet run the STFT variant in this model.”
